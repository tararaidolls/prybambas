Most inventions solve a **contradiction**: something must be one way *and* the opposite way.
An umbrella must be **big** in the rain and **small** in a bag. The solution? It folds.
Engineers call this way of thinking **TRIZ**, and kids can learn it at the kitchen table. Ages 5–14, 10 minutes, no materials.

## How to play

1. Pick any object at home.
2. Finish the sentence: "It must be ___, but also ___."
3. Find how the inventors solved it.

## Examples to start with

- **Umbrella:** big, but small → it *folds*.
- **Pencil:** must write, but mistakes must disappear → an *eraser on the other end*.
- **Backpack:** must hold a lot, but stay light → *soft fabric and many pockets*.
- **Drinking straw:** must be straight, but bend toward the mouth → a *bendy part*.

## Inventor's challenge

A school bag must be **big enough for all the books** but **easy to carry up the stairs**. Invent three solutions. (Hint: what else can split, fold, roll or change shape?)

## Questions for different ages

- **5–7:** What else in the kitchen folds or bends? Why?
- **8–10:** Find one object with a contradiction nobody has solved yet.
- **11–14:** Which trick did each inventor use: split it, nest it, merge it or change it over time?
