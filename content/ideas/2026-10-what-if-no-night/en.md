Ask this at dinner: **"What would change if the Sun never set?"**
It sounds like a fantasy, but every answer leads to the next one — and that is how kids learn to think in consequences. Ages 5–14, about 10 minutes.

## A possible chain of consequences

1. The Sun never sets → **it is always light**.
2. Always light → it is hard to fall asleep → **everyone needs thick curtains and eye masks**.
3. No cool nights → **the ground keeps warming up** → summers get much hotter.
4. Night animals (owls, bats, moths) lose their time → **they struggle to find food**.
5. No dark sky → **no stars** → sailors long ago could not have navigated by them.

There is no single correct chain. The rule is: *each step must follow from the one before*. Ask "why?" at every step.

## Does this happen anywhere for real?

Yes! Near the North and South Poles there is the **polar day**: in summer the Sun stays in the sky for weeks. People there sleep with blackout curtains, and some towns even hold sports events at midnight.

## Questions for different ages

- **5–7:** When would you go to bed? How would you know it's bedtime?
- **8–10:** Which animals would win, and which would lose? Why?
- **11–14:** What would happen to temperature, plants and electricity use?

## 5-minute experiment

Put two ice cubes on two plates. Keep one under a desk lamp and one in a dark place. Which melts first? Now link it back: what would "always light" do to the Earth?

> Tip: don't rush to the answer. The best part is the "why?" between the steps.
